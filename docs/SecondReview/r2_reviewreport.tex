\begin{reviewabstract}
\textbf{Background:} Early detection of Alzheimer's disease (AD) is critical to managing the
growing global dementia burden. Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging
(rs-fMRI) offers crucial insights into early functional brain network disruptions before
irreversible structural damage occurs. However, existing computational approaches face
major limitations: they often analyze functional biomarkers independently, omit patient
comorbidity profiles, ignore the continuous nature of biological progression, and lack
diagnostic uncertainty quantification.

\textbf{Objective:} We propose a novel framework, the Comorbidity-Aware Meta-Bayesian
Multi-Attention Transformer, to learn a shared, biomarker-invariant AD representation while
explicitly integrating clinical covariates and quantifying prediction uncertainty.

\textbf{Methods:} Resting-state fMRI scans were drawn from the Alzheimer's Disease
Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) repository. The data foundation was built first: 175
acquisitions were audited, 29 missing scans were recovered from raw DICOM and 2 were
excluded, and 165 acquisitions from 127 subjects were preprocessed with head-motion
correction, direct normalization to the MNI template, band-limited temporal filtering and a
27-regressor nuisance model. Slice-timing correction was not possible because the metadata
are not recoverable, and no T1-weighted image exists for any subject, so no anatomical
co-registration was used. Four complementary functional biomarkers were then
extracted---Amplitude of Low-Frequency Fluctuations (ALFF), Regional Homogeneity (ReHo),
Degree Centrality (DC), and Functional Connectivity (FC)---with the brain parcellated into
246 regions via the Brainnetome Atlas, and each was normalized within subject. The proposed
model will use a Multi-Attention Transformer (incorporating global, local, and network
attention) to learn critical region embeddings, and a meta-learning module to distill
shared, biomarker-invariant disease signatures. These imaging representations will be fused
with patient-specific clinical metadata (age, APOE $\varepsilon$4 genotype, MMSE, CDR-SB,
Geriatric Depression Scale scores, and cardiovascular and endocrine medical history). A
Bayesian ordinal classifier will then assign each subject a position along six sequentially
ordered cognitive stages spanning the healthy-to-diagnosed continuum---CN, SMC, EMCI, MCI,
LMCI, and AD---together with a calibrated confidence score for each prediction.

\textbf{Current Development and Evaluation Framework:} Modules 1 to 3 of the proposed system
(data, preprocessing and feature extraction) are complete, one milestone ahead of the
Review~I timeline. The modelling stages (Modules 4 to 6) have not been started, and no
performance result is reported. Every derived feature was verified independently, and
degree centrality recomputed from the stored connectivity matrices agreed exactly for all
165 acquisitions. Two findings shaped the design. FC strength, the per-region summary of FC,
is exactly DC$/245$, so all four biomarkers are kept as a $246\times3$ regional matrix plus
the full $246\times246$ connectivity matrix. Stage-wise group-mean heatmaps over 127 subjects
show no detectable univariate progression signal (1, 0 and 1 of 246 regions monotonic,
against about 0.7 expected by chance), which motivates a multivariate model. The model will
be judged on the standard classification metric suite (accuracy, precision, recall,
F1-score, ROC-AUC) alongside two model-specific checks: how well calibrated the Bayesian
uncertainty estimates are, and how meaningful the learned attention maps prove to be, under
subject-grouped cross-validation and against single-modality baselines.

\textbf{Significance and Conclusion:} This work introduces a novel architectural formulation
designed to advance transparent modeling of Alzheimer's disease progression. By uniquely
unifying cross-biomarker transferability, multi-attention networks, patient comorbidity
profiles, and probabilistic classification, this framework ultimately aims to deliver a
robust, highly explainable clinical decision support system.

\keywords{Alzheimer's Disease Progression, rs-fMRI Biomarkers, Multi-Attention Transformer,
Meta-Learning, Bayesian Ordinal Classification, Comorbidity Fusion}
\end{reviewabstract}

\chapter{Introduction}

Alzheimer's disease is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder and the leading
cause of dementia worldwide. Following the staging scheme fixed at Review~I, its
clinical course is divided into six ordered stages: cognitively normal (CN),
subjective memory concern (SMC), early mild cognitive impairment (EMCI), mild
cognitive impairment (MCI), late mild cognitive impairment (LMCI) and Alzheimer's
disease (AD). The boundaries between these
intermediate stages are diagnostically important, because therapeutic and care
decisions differ substantially between them, yet they are difficult to resolve from
cognitive testing alone. Distinguishing them reliably from non-invasive imaging is
therefore of direct clinical value.

Resting-state functional MRI measures spontaneous low-frequency fluctuations in the
blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal while the subject is at rest. Because
functional disruption in AD is believed to precede measurable structural atrophy,
rs-fMRI derived measures are attractive candidate markers of early progression.
Several complementary families of rs-fMRI measure exist. Amplitude of
low-frequency fluctuation (ALFF) quantifies the local strength of spontaneous
activity~\cite{alff}; regional homogeneity (ReHo) quantifies the local temporal
coherence of neighbouring voxels~\cite{reho}; functional connectivity (FC)
quantifies statistical dependence between distant regions; and degree centrality
(DC) summarises how strongly a region participates in the whole-brain
network~\cite{centrality}. Each captures a different aspect of the same underlying
physiology.

This diversity is both an opportunity and a difficulty. Individual biomarkers are
known to be noisy and to vary with acquisition protocol, so a model trained on one
alone tends to generalise poorly. The central premise of this project is that a
representation shared across several biomarkers --- one that is invariant to which
biomarker it was derived from --- should be more stable than any single measure. A
further complication is that AD rarely occurs in isolation: age, APOE4 genotype,
depression, hypertension and diabetes all modulate both brain function and cognitive
decline, and a model that ignores them attributes comorbidity-driven variation to
disease stage. Finally, because staging decisions are clinical, a point prediction
is insufficient; a calibrated measure of uncertainty is required for a prediction to
be actionable.

The significance of the present stage of work lies in the data foundation. Every
downstream modelling claim rests on the correctness of the preprocessing and
biomarker extraction beneath it, and on an honest account of what the source data
can and cannot support. Accordingly, this report emphasises verified, reproducible
processing with explicit documentation of every point at which the dataset was found
to be limiting.

\chapter{Problem statement}\label{ch:problem}

The problem statement is the one fixed at Review~I.

\medskip
\noindent\fbox{\parbox{0.94\textwidth}{\textbf{Problem.} Existing computational systems
analyse rs-fMRI biomarkers independently and omit clinical context, resulting in
overconfident deterministic staging errors on borderline subjects for diagnostic
clinicians.}}

\medskip
\noindent The primary problem addressed by this research is the development of a unified,
robust computational framework that can accurately assess continuous Alzheimer's
disease progression by synthesising heterogeneous multi-biomarker resting-state fMRI
data with patient-specific clinical characteristics and comorbidity metadata. Three
points frame it.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item \textbf{Context.} Dementia drives progressive cognitive decline across six
        ordered stages.
  \item \textbf{Limitation.} Current pipelines isolate complementary functional
        features (ALFF, ReHo, DC, FC).
  \item \textbf{Clinical gap.} Models ignore how patient comorbidities modify the
        underlying neurodegeneration.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Research question.} Can a common Alzheimer's progression representation be
discovered across multiple functional biomarkers while accounting for patient
comorbidity profiles and prediction uncertainty?

\section{Input Specifications}
The framework operates on a multifaceted patient vector extracted from the ADNI
cohort~\cite{adnimri,adniclinical}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item \textbf{Neuroimaging data:} cleaned, time-ordered BOLD signal recordings
        extracted from each subject's rs-fMRI scan.
  \item \textbf{Anatomical representation:} region-of-interest (ROI) matrices
        parcellated into 246 distinct functional areas using the Brainnetome Atlas.
  \item \textbf{Functional biomarkers:} four complementary regional descriptors
        --- ALFF, ReHo, FC and DC --- each capturing a distinct facet of local or
        network-level brain activity.
  \item \textbf{Clinical and comorbidity covariates:} age and APOE~$\varepsilon4$
        genotype status; MMSE and CDR-SB; hypertension history, diabetes mellitus
        profiling and Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) scores.
\end{itemize}
Diagnostic group labels are taken only from \texttt{participants.tsv}. The ADNI medical-history tables obtained carry only broad cardiovascular and endocrine categories, so the hypertension and diabetes covariates will be used under those category names (Section~\ref{sec:notdone}).

\section{Output Specifications}
The architecture maps the multi-modal inputs to a risk-calibrated output tuple:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item \textbf{6-class ordinal staging:} each subject is placed on the ordered scale
        CN $\rightarrow$ SMC $\rightarrow$ EMCI $\rightarrow$ MCI $\rightarrow$ LMCI
        $\rightarrow$ AD.
  \item \textbf{Probabilistic confidence:} a calibrated uncertainty metric output
        alongside each stage, flagging boundary cases and atypical trajectories.
  \item \textbf{Anatomical explainability:} localised attention heatmaps showing which
        brain regions and macro-networks (DMN, SN, ECN) drove the prediction.
\end{itemize}

\section{System Constraints}
The framework is bounded by the following, as set at Review~I:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item \textbf{High-dimensional feature spaces:} the spatiotemporal complexity of
        rs-fMRI requires dimensionality reduction without losing fine neural detail.
  \item \textbf{Scan variant irregularities:} multi-site scanner variance in ADNI
        demands standardised preprocessing.
  \item \textbf{Biomarker heterogeneity:} local synchronisation and global network
        centrality are different signal types and need a biomarker-invariant mapping.
  \item \textbf{Cross-modal alignment:} fusing high-dimensional imaging tensors with
        sparse scalar clinical metadata.
  \item \textbf{Computational burden:} the memory and training cost of cascaded global,
        local and network self-attention.
  \item \textbf{Calibration reliability:} the Bayesian ordinal output must give
        stable, well-calibrated uncertainty for borderline subjects.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Constraints encountered while building the data foundation}
Building Modules~1--3 added concrete limits that instantiate the above.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item \textbf{No anatomical reference.} Zero of the 131 audited subjects have a T1w
        image, so anatomical normalisation, subject-specific tissue segmentation and
        contrast-to-noise estimation are unavailable.
  \item \textbf{No slice-timing metadata.} Slice-timing correction cannot be applied to
        any acquisition.
  \item \textbf{Scan variant irregularities.} Eight acquisitions have a TR of about
        6.02\,s, which breaks the fixed 0.01--0.10\,Hz band; one further acquisition has
        TR 2.25\,s, which passes the Nyquist check (0.22\,Hz) and was processed normally.
  \item \textbf{Small effective sample.} 165 acquisitions come from only 127 distinct
        subjects, and the smallest group has 14. Model capacity and validation design
        are bounded by this, not by the acquisition count.
  \item \textbf{Single-worker compute.} All processing runs with one worker on a
        memory-constrained WSL2 environment, so the pipeline is checkpointed and
        resumable.
\end{itemize}

\section{Evaluation Criteria}
Model performance will be validated across quantitative, probabilistic and qualitative
dimensions, as fixed at Review~I:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item \textbf{Classification performance:} accuracy, precision, recall and macro-F1
        across all six stages.
  \item \textbf{Discriminative performance:} macro-averaged and per-class ROC-AUC.
  \item \textbf{Bayesian calibration:} expected calibration error (ECE), to test
        borderline-subject identification.
  \item \textbf{Explainability:} regional correlation of the learned attention vectors
        with established neurodegeneration atlases.
  \item \textbf{Benchmark comparison:} against independent single-modality baselines
        and standard clinical prediction architectures.
\end{itemize}
The criteria for the data stage reported here are: complete 246/246 regional coverage
for every acquisition; zero non-finite values in any derived feature; and every derived
quantity reproducible by independent recomputation to within $10^{-10}$. The model-stage
criteria above will be evaluated under subject-grouped cross-validation, and none has
been evaluated yet.

\chapter{Methodology}\label{ch:method}

The proposed system, the Comorbidity-Aware Meta-Bayesian Multi-Attention Transformer,
follows the six-module architecture fixed at Review~I: Data/Input Management,
Preprocessing Pipeline, Functional Feature Extraction (Brainnetome 246), Proposed Deep
Learning Model, Core Output Classifier and Decision Support Dashboard. Modules~2 and~3
are complete, Module~1 is complete for imaging only, and Modules~4--6 have not yet
been started.

\section{Proposed Workflow}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Step \arabic*:}]
  \item \emph{(Module 1)} Audit the raw cohort, recover acquisitions lacking image
        data from DICOM, document all exclusions, and sort the clinical tables.
  \item \emph{(Module 2)} Preprocess each acquisition into a common template space
        under the dataset's metadata constraints.
  \item \emph{(Module 3)} Parcellate into the Brainnetome-246 atlas, extract regional
        time series, and generate and verify ALFF, ReHo, FC and DC.
  \item \emph{(Module 3)} Normalise within subject, without using cross-subject or
        label information.
  \item \emph{(Module 4)} Encode the features with a multi-attention transformer, learn
        a shared biomarker-invariant representation by meta-learning, and fuse the
        comorbidity covariates.
  \item \emph{(Module 5)} Classify stage with a Bayesian ordinal head that reports
        uncertainty.
  \item \emph{(Module 6)} Present the stage, probabilities and attention maps in the
        decision-support dashboard.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Modules}
Each module is described by its purpose, inputs, processing, outputs and dependencies,
using the module names fixed at Review~I.

\subsection{Module 1: Data/Input Management}
\textbf{Purpose.} Gather, structure and sanitise the raw inputs, and establish a
trustworthy cohort with an explicit record of what the source data does and does not
contain. \textbf{Inputs.} Raw ADNI rs-fMRI scans (DICOM and NIfTI) and the clinical
tables. \textbf{Processing.} Inventory every acquisition; convert and recover
acquisitions lacking image data from DICOM; detect duplicate and truncated
acquisitions by checksum and header inspection; audit slice-timing recoverability;
organise into a BIDS-like layout with group membership taken only from
\texttt{participants.tsv}; sort the clinical tables (CDR, MMSE, GDS, medical history,
age, APOE~$\varepsilon4$). \textbf{Outputs.} Organised cohort, exclusion list and audit
reports; clinical tables bound to subjects. \textbf{Dependencies.} None.
\textbf{Status.} Complete for imaging; the clinical tables are obtained for five of six
groups but not yet cleaned, and the LMCI tables are missing (Section~\ref{sec:notdone}).

\subsection{Module 2: Preprocessing Pipeline}
\textbf{Purpose.} Neutralise cross-site and acquisition variance and isolate the
low-frequency BOLD signal, bringing every acquisition into a common template space.
\textbf{Inputs.} Module~1 cohort. \textbf{Processing.} The eleven steps of
Table~\ref{tab:preproc}. \textbf{Outputs.} One preprocessed 4D BOLD volume, brain mask,
motion parameter file, nuisance design matrix, provenance record and QC report per
acquisition. \textbf{Dependencies.} Module~1. \textbf{Status.} Complete for 165 of 173
eligible acquisitions.

\subsection{Module 3: Functional Feature Extraction (Brainnetome 246)}
\textbf{Purpose.} Translate the BOLD signal into a fixed 246-region representation and
four functional biomarkers. \textbf{Inputs.} Preprocessed BOLD from Module~2; the
Brainnetome atlas. \textbf{Processing.} Parcellation mapping onto each subject's own
grid with nearest-neighbour interpolation only; regional time-series extraction;
voxel-wise ALFF and ReHo aggregated to regions; ROI-level FC and DC
(Table~\ref{tab:biomarkers}); independent recomputation checks; and, as an addition to
the Review~I design, leakage-free within-subject normalisation
(Table~\ref{tab:norm}). \textbf{Outputs.} Per acquisition: the $135\times246$ ROI time
series, ALFF, ReHo and DC vectors, the $246\times246$ FC matrix and, after
normalisation, ALFF, ReHo and DC as a $246\times3$ regional matrix and FC as the
full Fisher-$z$ matrix. All four Review~I biomarkers are kept; Review~I assembled them
into a $246\times4$ matrix, whose FC column is covered in Section~\ref{sec:redundancy}.
\textbf{Dependencies.} Module~2. \textbf{Status.} Complete for 165 of 165 acquisitions.

\subsection{Module 4: Proposed Deep Learning Model}
\textbf{Purpose.} Encode multi-scale functional dependencies and distil shared,
biomarker-invariant disease representations. \textbf{Inputs.} Module~3 features and the
clinical covariates. \textbf{Processing.} Token encoding of the region features with a
Transformer; global, local and network self-attention~\cite{vaswani2017};
latent-representation learning; a meta-learning loop across biomarker
tasks~\cite{finn2017}; and multimodal fusion into $H=[Z'\,\|\,C]$. Subject-grouped
stratified cross-validation, with cross-subject scaling fitted on training folds only,
is part of this module's evaluation protocol. \textbf{Outputs.} Fused latent vector.
\textbf{Dependencies.} Module~3 and the cleaned clinical tables. \textbf{Status.} Not
started.

\subsection{Module 5: Core Output Classifier}
\textbf{Purpose.} Map the fused vector to a calibrated, uncertainty-aware ordinal stage.
\textbf{Inputs.} Fused vector from Module~4. \textbf{Processing.} An ordinal multi-layer
perceptron head over CN $\rightarrow$ SMC $\rightarrow$ EMCI $\rightarrow$ MCI
$\rightarrow$ LMCI $\rightarrow$ AD with a cumulative-link formulation~\cite{mccullagh1980},
\begin{equation}
  P(y \le k \mid H) = \sigma\!\left(\theta_k - g(H)\right), \qquad
  \theta_0 < \theta_1 < \dots < \theta_4,
\end{equation}
with Bayesian layers~\cite{gal2016} for predictive uncertainty and flagging of
borderline subjects. \textbf{Outputs.} Stage $\hat{y}$ with class probabilities and a
confidence score. \textbf{Dependencies.} Module~4. \textbf{Status.} Not started.

\subsection{Module 6: Decision Support Dashboard}
\textbf{Purpose.} Present the results to a clinician as analytical decision support
only, not as a replacement for professional evaluation. \textbf{Inputs.} Stage labels,
probabilities, attention indices and patient metadata from Modules~1, 3, 4 and~5.
\textbf{Processing.} Patient summary, progression chart, class probabilities,
explainability overlays and network (DMN, SN, ECN) visualisation. \textbf{Outputs.}
Interactive dashboard. \textbf{Dependencies.} Modules~1 and~5. \textbf{Status.} Not
started.

\textbf{Meta-learning across biomarkers.} Let $b\in\{\mathrm{ALFF},\mathrm{ReHo},
\mathrm{DC},\mathrm{FC}\}$ index the tasks, and let $f_\theta$ be the shared encoder. For each
task a \emph{support} set adapts the encoder and a \emph{query} set measures how well
the adapted encoder works, following model-agnostic meta-learning~\cite{finn2017}:
\begin{equation}
  \theta'_b = \theta - \alpha\,\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{sup}}_b(\theta),
  \qquad
  \theta \leftarrow \theta - \beta\,\nabla_\theta \sum_{b}\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{qry}}_b(\theta'_b).
\end{equation}
The outer update rewards parameters $\theta$ from which a few steps work well on
\emph{every} biomarker, which is what makes $Z'=f_\theta(X)$ biomarker-invariant. Support
and query sets are drawn from different subjects, and all of them come from the training
folds only. The tasks here are biomarkers, not sites or subjects.

\textbf{Bayesian ordinal head.} For the fused vector $H$ the head scores
$g_w(H)$ and uses ordered thresholds $\theta_0<\dots<\theta_4$ (the cumulative-link
form~\cite{mccullagh1980}):
\begin{equation}
  P(y\le k\mid H,w)=\sigma\big(\theta_k-g_w(H)\big),\qquad k=0,\dots,4 .
\end{equation}
The weights have a prior and an approximate posterior $q(w)$, obtained for example by
variational inference or Monte Carlo dropout~\cite{gal2016}. The predictive probability
averages over $S$ weight samples $w_s\sim q(w)$:
\begin{equation}
  \bar p_k=\frac1S\sum_{s=1}^{S}\Big[P(y\le k\mid H,w_s)-P(y\le k-1\mid H,w_s)\Big],
\end{equation}
with $P(y\le -1)=0$ and $P(y\le 5)=1$. Uncertainty is the predictive entropy
$U=-\sum_k \bar p_k\log\bar p_k$. A subject is flagged as borderline when two adjacent
stages both carry substantial probability.

\textbf{Status and open choices.} Nothing here has been trained. First-order or full
meta-gradients, variational inference or Monte Carlo dropout, and the size of $S$ are not
yet fixed. The model will be evaluated with the Review~I metrics, including expected
calibration error.

\chapter{Implementation}\label{ch:impl}

All processing ran under WSL2 (Ubuntu) on Windows 11 with Python 3.12.3, NumPy
2.3.5, SciPy 1.15.3, NiBabel 5.4.2, Nilearn 0.14.1, ANTsPy 0.6.3, TemplateFlow
25.1.2 and FreeSurfer 7.4.1. Every long-running job used a single worker, with all
thread-count environment variables pinned to one.

\section{Module 1: Data/Input Management}

The initial audit covered 131 subjects and found 72 valid NIfTI files against 103
records that had JSON metadata but no image data at the audited location. Of these, 29
were recovered by converting raw DICOM archives (9 in the MCI group and 20 in the
SMC group, the latter representing 62.5\% of that group's final data), with no
conversion failures; the remaining records were reconciled against NIfTI files found
by the later per-group mapping audits. A dataset-wide checksum audit of 175 image files found no
orphans and exactly one hash collision, a subject exported twice under different
series numbers; the duplicate was excluded and the original retained. A second
acquisition was excluded for containing only 7 volumes where all others contain 140,
a truncation traced to the source export rather than to any processing step. Both
exclusions are recorded in a canonical exclusion list that every batch script
consults.

An early working note recorded six AD acquisitions as unrecoverable. This figure was
re-checked directly against the current source data before the production run and
found to be stale: all 20 AD subjects and all 25 AD acquisitions were present and
valid, and all were processed.

The slice-timing audit examined all 175 JSON sidecars and representative DICOM
headers spanning all 17 distinct protocol signatures in the archive, inspecting
private vendor groups as well as standard tags. No sidecar contained a
\texttt{SliceTiming} field. Within the DICOM headers, \texttt{AcquisitionTime} and
\texttt{TriggerTime} were identical across all slices sampled within a volume,
confirming volume-level rather than slice-level timing granularity. A Philips private
tag provides a per-slice \emph{spatial} index, but no tag anywhere states the
temporal acquisition order, and the spatial index was deliberately not used to infer
it. No multiband tag was found among decodable names. The audit's conclusion was
recorded as \texttt{SLICE\_TIMING\_NOT\_RECOVERABLE}, and no timing value was
inferred or fabricated at any point.

The clinical half of Module~1 is less advanced than the imaging half. Cohort table
archives (CDR, MMSE, GDS and medical history) have been obtained for five of the six
groups, but they have not yet been parsed or cleaned, and the LMCI tables are missing
(Section~\ref{sec:notdone}). A further 50 acquisitions per stage are planned; they have
not yet been downloaded or processed.

Two implementation points deserve emphasis. First, because no T1w image exists,
white-matter and CSF candidate regressors are derived from published population
tissue probability priors rather than from subject-specific segmentation; a candidate
is included only if its mask retains at least 20 voxels and its correlation with the
global signal is below 0.98 in magnitude. The white-matter regressor passed this gate
in all 165 acquisitions and the CSF regressor failed it in all 165, so the nuisance
design matrix has exactly 27 columns in every acquisition (intercept, 24 Friston
terms, white matter, global signal); every provenance record states
\texttt{CSF\_status = NOT PERFORMED}. The ventricular CSF prior is too thin to survive
at 4\,mm: the corresponding 2\,mm pilot mask held only 111 voxels (888\,mm$^3$)
after thresholding at 0.90 and intersection with the brain mask, which is roughly 14
voxels on the 4\,mm grid, below the 20-voxel minimum. The voxel count and correlation
at the gate were not stored per acquisition, so this is the explanation consistent
with the logged evidence rather than a per-acquisition measurement. No substitute
CSF regressor was introduced. Second, contrast-to-noise ratio is reported as not reliably
computable for all 165 acquisitions, because it requires a tissue segmentation this
dataset cannot provide (no T1w image exists for any of the 131 subjects); no
segmentation was fabricated to produce a number.

The band-pass step validates, per acquisition, that the requested upper cutoff lies
below the Nyquist frequency implied by that acquisition's own TR. Eight acquisitions
failed this check. All eight share a TR of approximately 6.02\,s, roughly double the
3.0\,s used elsewhere in the cohort, giving a Nyquist limit near 0.083\,Hz --- below
the 0.10\,Hz cutoff. These were refused rather than silently reprocessed with an
altered band, since changing the filter for a subset would make those acquisitions
non-comparable with the rest. Seven of the eight have a TR of 6.02158\,s and one has
6.02353\,s; all eight belong to site~002 (subject identifiers \texttt{002\_S\_xxxx}).

\section{Module 3: Functional Feature Extraction (Brainnetome 246)}

\subsection{Parcellation and Regional Time-Series Extraction}

The Brainnetome atlas~\cite{brainnetome} is supplied at 2\,mm in LAS orientation
whereas the
preprocessed BOLD is RAS, so the atlas is first reoriented to canonical orientation,
a lossless array operation involving no interpolation, verified by per-label voxel
count equality. It is then resampled onto each subject's own BOLD grid using
\emph{nearest-neighbour interpolation only}. This is essential: atlas values are
categorical region identifiers, and linear or spline interpolation would produce
fractional labels with no meaning. Regional time series are extracted by direct index
masking on the verified-identical grid rather than through a masker utility that
might silently re-resample the labels a second time.

ALFF and ReHo are computed at voxel level and only then aggregated to regions,
rather than computed from the regional time series, so that local spatial
information is used where the definitions require it. The 27-voxel ReHo neighbourhood
is the original formulation~\cite{reho} and the default of established toolboxes,
chosen for comparability rather than arbitrarily. Because the preprocessed BOLD has already been
band-pass filtered, ALFF is computed on an already band-limited spectrum; this is a
documented consequence of the fixed pipeline rather than a change of method.

The connectivity matrix is retained in full. No threshold, binarisation or absolute
value is applied, and negative correlations are preserved, since thresholding would
impose an unvalidated choice and discard the sign information that distinguishes
anti-correlated from unconnected regions.

A single-acquisition pilot was implemented and validated first. Before scaling to the
cohort, the production implementation was required to reproduce that pilot: ALFF,
ReHo, regional time series, atlas labels and regional voxel counts were recovered
bit-identically, and FC and DC agreed to within $1.8 \times 10^{-15}$. Only then was
the pilot's own output directory protected and the remaining acquisitions processed.

\subsection{Feature Redundancy: FC Strength and DC}\label{sec:redundancy}

Auditing our own features, rather than inheriting them from the design, exposed an
exact redundancy. The pipeline stores a regional connectivity strength defined as the
\emph{mean} of each region's off-diagonal connectivity, while DC is the \emph{sum}
over the same 245 entries, so
\[
  \mathrm{FCS}_i \;=\; \frac{1}{245}\sum_{j\neq i}\mathrm{FC}_{ij}

\section{Engineering for an Unstable Execution Environment}

Early production runs failed repeatedly, and diagnosis of the failure mode
materially shaped the implementation. The symptom was that the virtual machine
terminated after roughly five to twelve acquisitions with no Python traceback. The
cause was sustained small-granularity array input/output against the Windows
drive mount, which produced \texttt{Errno 5} input/output errors and virtual machine
level failures under load. Three changes resolved it. Each acquisition is now staged
on the native Linux filesystem and its results copied to the Windows-visible tree
only once complete, so a crash never leaves a partially written output directory.
Resume validation reads array shapes through memory mapping and file headers instead
of loading every finished acquisition back into memory, which had been re-reading
gigabytes on every restart. Finally, a wrapper distinguishes a genuine Python error,
which stops the run for inspection, from a virtual machine failure, which triggers a
restart and resumption from the last validated checkpoint. Restart count fell from
23 to 2 for the same workload.

Checkpointing re-validates the actual files on disk --- shapes, finiteness and
structural invariants --- before treating any acquisition as complete, so the
checkpoint record alone is never trusted.

The result (Table~\ref{tab:monotonic}) is negative and is reported as such, in four steps.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item We tested for a univariate, per-region group-mean progression signal.
  \item It is not detectable. Only 1, 0 and 1 of 246 regions are monotonic from CN to
        AD, which is no more than the roughly 0.7 that chance alone gives, and the
        variation across stages is about 4--5 times smaller than the anatomical
        variation between regions (ratios 0.18--0.24).
  \item This is informative, not a failure of the pipeline. Per-region stage means
        discard the entire $246\times246$ connectivity structure, and they can only
        reveal effects large enough to survive averaging over 14--26 subjects per
        stage. The result does not show that the biomarkers carry no stage
        information; it shows that group means of single regions do not expose it.
  \item The multivariate architecture is therefore motivated and not assumed: the
        transformer sees each subject individually and all 246 regions jointly, with
        connectivity intact.
\end{enumerate}

tested on any classification task.

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{Comparison with conventional practice.} The pipeline departs from a textbook
rs-fMRI workflow at two points, in both cases because the data compels it. A
conventional pipeline co-registers BOLD to a subject T1w image and normalises
through it, and derives tissue regressors from that subject's own segmentation.
Neither is possible here, so EPI-to-template registration is performed directly and
tissue regressors are taken from population priors. This is a genuine limitation
rather than a simplification: direct EPI registration is less precise because EPI
has lower anatomical contrast, and its quality cannot be benchmarked against a
T1w-mediated alternative using this dataset. Equally, a conventional pipeline applies
slice-timing correction; the exhaustive audit described in Chapter~\ref{ch:impl}
establishes this is not available, and the honest response was to omit the step and
document it rather than assume an acquisition order.

\textbf{On scale and feature redundancy.} Two findings will shape the modelling
stage more than any preprocessing choice. First, raw ALFF varies by more than two
orders of magnitude across acquisitions, because it is expressed in units
proportional to raw BOLD intensity, whereas ReHo --- being bounded --- varies only
narrowly. Supplying both unnormalised to a model would allow scanner and protocol
scaling to dominate any biological signal; the within-subject normalisation in
Module~3 addresses this. Second, the per-region summary of FC, the fourth column of the
Review~I matrix, was found to be an exact rescaling of DC. All four biomarkers are
still used: ALFF, ReHo and DC as three regional features, and FC in full as a
connectivity matrix. Both findings were identified by inspecting the data rather than inherited
from the design, which illustrates the value of auditing features before modelling.

\textbf{Limitations.} Beyond the absence of T1w and slice-timing data, four
limitations bear on the conclusions that can eventually be drawn. The effective
sample is 127 subjects across six ordered classes with a smallest class of 14,
(a further 50 acquisitions per stage are planned and will change these counts), which bounds model capacity and makes a single held-out test split uninformative;
repeated subject-grouped cross-validation is required. No cross-site or cross-scanner
harmonisation was applied (Table~\ref{tab:deviations}), so site and protocol effects may remain in the features. Eight acquisitions are
permanently excluded on account of the 6.02\,s TR subgroup unless the filter
specification is revisited for them. Finally, the comorbidity data are incomplete and the gap is group-structured; see
Section~\ref{sec:notdone}, which also records what has not yet been done.

\textbf{Mitigation: dataset expansion.} The main limitation above is the small effective
sample. A further 50 acquisitions per stage (about 300 in total) are planned to address it.
The limitation counts subjects, not acquisitions: 165 acquisitions come from 127 subjects,
and MCI has 32 acquisitions from only 14 subjects. The expansion therefore helps in
proportion to the number of \emph{new subjects} it adds. If every added acquisition came from
a new subject the stages would grow by up to 50 subjects each (MCI from 14 to at most 64),
so the added acquisitions will be drawn from subjects not already in the cohort wherever
ADNI allows, and counts will be reported per subject. The expansion reuses the frozen
Modules~1 to~3 unchanged, and it has not yet started.

\textbf{Next steps.} Immediate work is to complete the comorbidity table, construct
the subject-level cohort index with fixed stratified grouped cross-validation folds
so that cross-subject scaling can be fitted on training data only, and then
implement the multi-attention transformer and meta-learning blocks. The ordinal
scale used by the classifier is the one fixed at Review~I, namely
CN $\rightarrow$ SMC $\rightarrow$ EMCI $\rightarrow$ MCI $\rightarrow$ LMCI
$\rightarrow$ AD, which orders the three mild-cognitive-impairment cohorts as
early, intermediate and late. This ordering determines the loss structure of the
ordinal model and is therefore held fixed throughout.

\chapter{Conclusion}\label{ch:conclusion}

This report establishes the data foundation of the Comorbidity-Aware Meta-Bayesian
framework. Of 175 ADNI rs-fMRI acquisitions audited, 165 from 127 subjects were
preprocessed with a single frozen pipeline, parcellated into the 246 Brainnetome regions,
turned into ALFF, ReHo, DC and FC biomarkers, and normalised within subject. Every derived
value was verified independently, including an exact recomputation of degree centrality for
all 165 acquisitions. Every limitation of the BOLD-only dataset, from the missing T1w images
and slice-timing metadata to the eight scans whose 6.02\,s TR breaks the fixed band, was
handled by documented exclusion or substitution and not by fabrication.

Two findings shaped the design. First, the per-region FC summary of Review~I is exactly
DC$/245$, so all four biomarkers are kept but enter the model as a $246\times3$ regional
matrix and a $246\times246$ Fisher-$z$ connectivity matrix. Second, stage-wise group-mean
heatmaps show no detectable univariate progression signal, which motivates the
multivariate model of Modules~4 and~5 instead of weakening it.

On the Review~I timeline, Modules~2 and~3 are complete ahead of schedule, while
Module~1 is complete for imaging only. Modules~4 to~6 have not been started, and no
accuracy, AUC or other performance figure exists. The next steps are to obtain the missing
LMCI tables and clean the clinical data, build the subject-grouped cross-validation folds,
enlarge the dataset by a further 50 acquisitions per stage, and then implement the
meta-learning and Bayesian ordinal model (Section~\ref{sec:revplan}).
